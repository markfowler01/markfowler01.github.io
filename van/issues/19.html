New windshield passed static cal. The highway said no.
From the Van · Issue #19

A Subaru Outback came in for a windshield after a rock did its thing on I-5.

Glass swapped, EyeSight static calibration run, targets read clean, no codes. On paper it was done.

Then the driver got it on the highway and lane centering wandered. Pulled right on a straight road. No lamp, no DTC, nothing to point at.

Here's the trap. Subaru EyeSight uses a stereo camera, two eyes behind that glass. It is brutally sensitive to the windshield in front of it. Aftermarket glass can have a slightly different optical zone or bracket angle than OE. The static cal will happily pass because the targets are close enough in a controlled bay. The road is not controlled.

Subaru is clear: any collision repair gets the required calibration, and any windshield touch means a full static EyeSight calibration. But "it passed" and "it works at 65" are two different sentences.

We pulled it, confirmed the aftermarket glass was the variable, and got OE glass in. Re-ran the cal. Centered straight after that.

Here's what that means for you: a passed static cal on an EyeSight car is not proof the car drives right. If the glass wasn't OE, you can own a comeback you never saw coming, and the first person the driver calls is the shop that did the windshield.

Ask your cal shop this week: "On EyeSight windshields, do you confirm the glass is OE-equivalent before you certify the calibration passed?"

The van has met aftermarket Subaru glass before. The van does not trust it.
